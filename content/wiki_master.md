## Urban Economics
- NIMBYism: Local opposition to housing, transit, or infrastructure that is seen as protecting neighborhood character or property values, even when the project serves a wider public need.
- Land Use Regulation: Zoning, setback, and approval rules that shape where homes and businesses can be built, often affecting supply, price, and neighborhood form.
- Gentrification: A shift in neighborhood investment and residents that can raise amenities and property values while also displacing long-time households and changing local culture.
- Agglomeration Economies: Productivity gains that arise when firms, workers, and services cluster together and share labor pools, customers, and ideas.

## Public Finance
- Pigouvian Tax: A tax designed to make a private actor pay for external costs such as pollution, congestion, or other harms not reflected in market prices.
- Fiscal Federalism: The way responsibilities, taxes, and transfers are divided across municipal, provincial, and national governments.
- User Fees: Charges tied to direct service use, often used for transit, utilities, or roads when policymakers want demand to face clearer price signals.
- Capital Budgeting: The practice of separating long-lived infrastructure spending from day-to-day operating costs so investments can be planned and financed more transparently.

## Macroeconomics
- Inflation Targeting: A central bank framework that uses an explicit inflation goal to anchor expectations and guide interest-rate decisions.
- Output Gap: The difference between actual economic activity and the economy's potential level, often used to gauge slack and inflation pressure.
- Recession: A broad and sustained decline in economic activity, typically visible in output, employment, income, and spending.
- Interest Rate Transmission: The channel through which central bank rates affect borrowing costs, asset prices, demand, and ultimately inflation.

## Labor Markets
- Labor Force Participation: The share of working-age people who are employed or actively looking for work, often used to assess labor market health.
- Minimum Wage: A legal wage floor intended to raise pay at the bottom of the distribution, with effects that depend on labor demand, productivity, and enforcement.
- Human Capital: The skills, health, and experience that make workers more productive and influence their earnings over time.
- Occupational Licensing: Rules that require workers to obtain credentials before practicing a job, which can raise quality but also limit entry.

## Trade and Industrial Policy
- Comparative Advantage: The idea that places benefit from specializing in what they produce relatively efficiently and trading for the rest.
- Tariff: A tax on imported goods that can protect domestic producers but often raises prices for consumers and downstream firms.
- Industrial Policy: Government efforts to shape the structure of production through subsidies, procurement, tax credits, or strategic regulation.
- Supply Chain Resilience: The ability of firms and economies to absorb shocks by diversifying suppliers, inventories, and logistics routes.

## Behavioral Economics
- Present Bias: The tendency to overvalue immediate rewards relative to future ones, helping explain procrastination and under-saving.
- Loss Aversion: The pattern in which losses feel larger than equally sized gains, often shaping consumer and investor behavior.
- Anchoring: The tendency to rely too heavily on an initial reference point when making judgments or forecasts.
- Social Norms: Shared expectations about behavior that can influence compliance, consumption, saving, and cooperation even without formal enforcement.
